# Vector Calculus Notes: Parametric Surfaces

**Prerequisite Concept: Parameterization**

In mathematics, parameterization means expressing the coordinates of a set of points as functions of independent variables called "parameters."

- **1 Parameter ($t$):** Creates a **curve** (a 1D string bent in 3D space). Example: $\mathbf{r}(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t) \rangle$.
    
- **2 Parameters ($u, v$):** Creates a **surface** (a 2D sheet bent, stretched, or twisted in 3D space).
    

## 1. The Core Concept

A **parametric surface** is a surface in 3D space defined by a vector-valued function of _two_ independent variables, typically denoted as $u$ and $v$.

Instead of defining a surface with a traditional Cartesian equation like $z = f(x, y)$ (which only works well if the surface doesn't fold back over itself), a parametric representation allows you to map a flat, 2D rectangular domain directly into 3D space. Think of it like taking a flat sheet of graph paper (the $uv$-plane) and bending, stretching, or rolling it into a 3D shape (the $xyz$-space).

### The Mathematics

The position vector $\mathbf{r}$ for any point on the surface is given by:

$$\mathbf{r}(u, v) = x(u, v)\hat{i} + y(u, v)\hat{j} + z(u, v)\hat{k}$$

Where $u$ and $v$ are restricted to a specific 2D domain $D$.

## 2. Grid Curves (Visualizing the Surface)

To understand how the flat $uv$-plane maps to the 3D surface, we use **grid curves**.

- **Hold $u$ constant (e.g., $u = c_1$):** By fixing $u$ and letting $v$ vary, the equation $\mathbf{r}(c_1, v)$ becomes a function of only one variable. This traces out a single 1D curve on the 3D surface.
    
- **Hold $v$ constant (e.g., $v = c_2$):** By fixing $v$ and letting $u$ vary, $\mathbf{r}(u, c_2)$ traces out an intersecting curve across the surface.
    
- By plotting many of these constant-$u$ and constant-$v$ curves, you create a wireframe mesh that visualizes the entire parametric surface.
    

## 3. Examples of Parametric Surfaces

### Example 1: A Cylinder

How do we mathematically "roll" a flat sheet into a tube? We use sine and cosine for the circular base, and a linear parameter for the height.

Let the radius be a constant $R$. We will use parameters $u$ (to represent the angle around the circle) and $v$ (to represent the height).

- $x(u, v) = R\cos(u)$
    
- $y(u, v) = R\sin(u)$
    
- $z(u, v) = v$
    

**The Vector Function:**

$$\mathbf{r}(u, v) = R\cos(u)\hat{i} + R\sin(u)\hat{j} + v\hat{k}$$

**The Domain:**

- $0 \le u \le 2\pi$ (This ensures we go exactly one full circle around the z-axis).
    
- $0 \le v \le h$ (This dictates the total height $h$ of the cylinder).
    

**Grid Curves here:** If you hold $v$ constant (a fixed height), the curve is a horizontal circle. If you hold $u$ constant (a fixed angle), the curve is a vertical straight line up the side of the cylinder.

### Example 2: A Sphere

A traditional equation for a sphere is $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = R^2$. To parameterize it, we borrow from spherical coordinates, using two angles as our parameters: $u$ (longitude, from $0$ to $2\pi$) and $v$ (latitude, from the north pole down to the south pole, $0$ to $\pi$).

- $x(u, v) = R\sin(v)\cos(u)$
    
- $y(u, v) = R\sin(v)\sin(u)$
    
- $z(u, v) = R\cos(v)$
    

**The Vector Function:**

$$\mathbf{r}(u, v) = R\sin(v)\cos(u)\hat{i} + R\sin(v)\sin(u)\hat{j} + R\cos(v)\hat{k}$$

**The Domain:**

- $0 \le u \le 2\pi$ (sweeping entirely around the equator)
    
- $0 \le v \le \pi$ (sweeping from the top pole to the bottom pole)
    

**Grid Curves here:** Holding $v$ constant gives horizontal circles (lines of latitude). Holding $u$ constant gives vertical half-circles passing through the poles (lines of longitude).

## 4. Why Use Parametric Surfaces? (The Normal Vector)

Parametric surfaces make it incredibly easy to calculate the **normal vector** (the vector pointing perfectly perpendicular to the surface at any given point). This is crucial for calculating surface area, flux (how much fluid passes through the surface), and lighting in 3D computer graphics.

To find the normal vector $\mathbf{n}$, you take the partial derivatives of $\mathbf{r}$ with respect to $u$ and $v$ (which give you two tangent vectors on the surface) and compute their cross product:

$$\mathbf{n} = \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial v}$$

$$\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v = \det \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial u} \\ \frac{\partial x}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial y}{\partial v} & \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \end{vmatrix}$$

The length of this resulting vector $||\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v||$ tells you how much the original 2D $uv$-area gets stretched when it is mapped onto the 3D surface, which is exactly how surface integrals are calculated.
